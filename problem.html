01 · The problem

Uganda's waste goes unsorted

Unsorted waste pollutes soil and water, spreads disease and sends recoverable material to landfill. The bottleneck is sorting.

The scale
43.7%

180 t

Councils face persistent financial and logistical limits, and African countries struggle to put waste policy into practice.

Where sorting happens, it is by hand, after collection: slow, inconsistent and hard to scale. In Kasese, better collection left sorting at source untouched.

Objectives

A low-cost bin that classifies, sorts and monitors

SO1Classify

Identify and evaluate a computer-vision model for four waste categories (accuracy, precision, recall, F1).

SO2Monitor

IoT fill-level sensing that tracks each chamber and signals when it needs emptying.

SO3Sort

An automated mechanism that routes each classified item into its chamber.

The opportunity

Sort at source, not after collection

Mixed waste loses its value in landfill. Sorting at the point of disposal sends each stream where it can be recovered.

  • Recyclables keep their value: clean plastic and metal can be sold and reprocessed.
  • Organic waste can be composted instead of rotting in landfill.
  • Collection follows need: per-chamber fill levels replace fixed schedules.
  • Start small: one building is a practical, replicable entry point for institutions.
Gap

The technology works elsewhere, but no one has tested whether a low-cost bin that classifies, sorts and monitors waste works in a Ugandan institution.

PRISMA 2020 review · Scopus & Google Scholar · 2015–2026 · 18 studies included.

G1Lab accuracy does not transfer

Reported 83–99.5%, but one model fell from 97.9% to 73.5% on a harder dataset; a clean-image model reached 49.7% on real landfill waste.

G2Few integrated systems

A practical bin must classify on cheap hardware, route items reliably and report fill levels. Most systems do only part of this.

G3No evidence from the region

None of the reviewed studies was implemented or evaluated in Uganda or East Africa.
